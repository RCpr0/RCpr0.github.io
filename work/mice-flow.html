Mice Flow · Solo build

Shipping a whole game alone, with AI tools

Mice Flow is a voxel puzzle where a colony of mice eats a picture block by block. I built all of it myself: design, code, levels and 3D art. I used Claude and Codex as coding partners and Meshy for 3D models, then got it running at near 60 fps on a budget phone.

RoleSolo developer
PlatformAndroid · Google Play
Year2026
ToolsUnity · Claude · Codex · Meshy
The challenge

A studio's worth of roles, one person

A shippable mobile game normally needs a designer, programmers, a 3D artist and a tech artist. I wanted to find out how far one designer could get with AI tools in every one of those seats, without lowering the bar on feel or performance.

How I built it
01 — Design

A picture you eat, one colour at a time

Each level is a voxel picture. Tapping a mouse house sends its mice out to eat the matching colour, so the order you tap in is the puzzle. I designed the core loop, boosters, onboarding and a 50-level opening pack, then tuned each level's composition and pacing.

02 — AI-assisted production

Directing tools like a team

I treated the AI tools the way a lead treats a team: write the spec, review the output, reject what isn't right.

  • Claude and Codex wrote code against my specs and reviews, with automated tests as the gate. More than 200 editor tests guard the project.
  • Meshy generated the 3D models. I set the art direction and refined the results in Unity.
  • Every visual change was checked against captures from the device, not just the editor.
Performance deep-dive

From 30 to 59 fps on a budget phone

The art direction worked, but the boards were far too heavy for a budget phone. I fixed it in three measured steps on a Pixel 4a, using release builds and a bot playing each level, with 30-second timed segments.

13×Fewer triangles on the densest board: 3.7M down to 283K
~60fps with flat materials, which proved shading was the real cost
29.7 → 59.1fps on the densest level after shader specialisation
≤ 2/255Maximum colour difference in matched captures, so no visible art change
  1. Geometry. Sculpted cheese tiles were 12,290 triangles each. Swapping them for baked tiles took the densest board from 3.7M to 283K triangles and from 901 to 281 draw calls. That brought it up to about 30 fps, still well short of 60.
  2. Diagnosis. With every material replaced by a flat colour, the same boards ran at about 60 fps. The geometry was now fine, and shading was the bottleneck.
  3. Shader specialisation. I removed per-pixel clipping from ordinary opaque draws, moved plain surfaces to vertex lighting, and added dedicated shader variants for cheese and houses that strip unused code. Levels 1–9 now run at 59.8–59.9 fps and the densest level at 59.1.
