KittyDoku + CarDoku · Puzzle design

Deduction puzzles you solve by looking

KittyDoku and CarDoku are logic puzzles in the family of Star Battle and Queens. I designed their rules and levels in a purpose-built HTML workbench with its own solver, so every level has exactly one answer and can be reached without guessing.

RolePrimary designer
GamesKittyDoku, CarDoku
Year2026
ToolsJavaScript workbench · Unity
The problem

Logic puzzles live or die on fairness

Players forgive hard puzzles. What they don't forgive is a puzzle that makes them guess, or a level with two valid answers where the game says one of them is wrong. A generator alone doesn't solve this: it can produce boards that are technically solvable but only through trial and error.

I wanted every level to be findable by looking. Each placement should be the last legal square left in its colour, never a move that only follows from imagining how a wrong guess would play out.

What I built
01 — The rule set

A car is two squares long

Place one car in every coloured region. A car stands on the square you tap and fills the square it faces, and its gaze must never reach another car. That one change, giving each piece a facing, turns placement into aiming: two facings on the same square are two different boards.

Later levels add mice (a car that looks at one is lost), water (out of play, but gazes cross it), and walls that block a gaze.

02 — The workbench

Solver, generator and lives in one page

The workbench is a dependency-free HTML game backed by a solver and a level generator. It enforces the design rules automatically:

  • Exactly one answer, counting facing as part of the answer.
  • No guessing. From level 7, where a car first chooses between two directions, every wrong direction is one the board visibly forbids.
  • Fair punishment. A wrong car costs one of three lives, but a move the game wouldn't allow is refused, not charged.
  • Mistakes explained in a speech bubble on the square, naming the colour a wrong car really blocks.
03 — Boards that fit a phone

Size set by packing, not taste

A car takes two squares, so the number that fit on a board has a hard ceiling. I measured it for every board shape and facing pool. Five columns is the minimum for an east-facing car with a neighbour, so the game opens on 5×5 rather than 4×4 (which only fits two cars), and boards top out at 5×7 so they fit a phone held upright.

04 — Benchmarking

Measured against the published genre leader

I transcribed 100 levels of a published game in the genre from a gameplay recording, ran them through an independent solver written to that game's stated rules, and put both catalogues through the same difficulty measurement. The result: despite the shared theme, they are different puzzles. Theirs has no facings and no hazards. That told us which of our difficulty levers had no equivalent in theirs, and where we had to set our own bar.

Result

Two shipped games from one rule engine

45Levels in the workbench design set, each with exactly one answer
446Automated tests on rules, solver and levels
2Games shipped on Google Play from the design
0Levels that need a guess to solve

What I'd do next: log the time each deduction takes in live play, and use it as a difficulty measure in place of board size and hazard count.
